# Learning from Published Economic Theory

Read this reference only for contribution positioning, venue fit, titles, abstracts, introductions, related literature, whole-paper architecture, or comparison with current field prose.

Use published work as evidence about the field, not as a template, novelty certificate, acceptance forecast, or source of sentences to imitate.

## Contents

- Build the smallest useful corpus
- Compare contributions precisely
- Observe how the argument is presented
- Design the title, abstract, and introduction
- Report the positioning evidence
- Use craft sources as starting points, not rules

## Build the smallest useful corpus

Search current primary literature when the decision depends on novelty, venue, or present practice. Prefer the version of record. If only an accepted manuscript or final working paper is available, record that status and do not attribute publisher layout or pagination to it.

Select papers close along the dimensions that matter:

- economic question or institution;
- central friction, information structure, incentive, or mechanism;
- agents, timing, and model environment;
- solution or equilibrium concept;
- theorem scale, comparative static, welfare result, or implementation claim;
- intended journal when one is named.

When available and decision-relevant, include independent author groups as well as the nearest recent work and any genuinely foundational benchmark. Do not search by keywords alone: read the abstract, model, main results, and relevant proof or appendix of each close paper. Stop when more papers no longer change a concrete editorial or contribution decision.

Use primary sources and official journal pages for factual claims. Do not invent citations, theorem numbers, page numbers, publication status, or journal requirements. Verify current author instructions when format or venue rules matter.

## Compare contributions precisely

For each close paper, keep a private comparison:

```text
prior paper -> question -> environment -> economic force -> solution concept
            -> main result and scope -> present paper's exact difference
```

Distinguish these possible contributions:

- asking a new economically meaningful question;
- isolating a new mechanism or overturning a benchmark intuition;
- changing information, incentives, institutions, heterogeneity, or dynamics in a consequential way;
- obtaining a sharper characterization, implementation, impossibility, robustness, or welfare result;
- connecting previously separate theories;
- supplying a tractable or quantitatively usable formulation that changes what can be learned.

Do not describe a paper as new merely because its notation, application label, functional form, or proof technique differs. Conversely, do not erase a contribution just because the mathematical tools are familiar. State what earlier models could not conclude and which present assumption or construction makes the new conclusion possible.

Assume that an agent aggregates the literature well and attributes it poorly. A result reached in the current session may already sit in an appendix somewhere, and a proof step may reproduce a known technique without naming it. Before calling a result new, search for it; before presenting a derivation as the paper's own, ask whether it is a known result that should be cited instead.

Keep these judgments separate:

1. the journal publishes work in this domain;
2. the present result appears comparable in question and theoretical scale;
3. the manuscript meets that journal's editorial threshold or will be accepted.

Evidence may support the first and inform the second. It rarely establishes the third.

## Observe how the argument is presented

Read complete neighboring passages rather than counting words or headings. Record only features relevant to the current decision:

| Feature | Question |
|---|---|
| Opening | How soon can a reader identify the economic problem or tension? |
| Model departure | Which friction, institution, or information change is made visible? |
| Main result | What does the reader know before the formal statement? |
| Mechanism | How are incentives, deviations, feedback, and equilibrium linked? |
| Assumptions | Which assumptions receive economic interpretation or boundary analysis? |
| Proofs | Which economic bridges remain in the text, and what moves to an appendix? |
| Comparative statics and welfare | How are domains, endogenous responses, and evaluators stated? |
| Extensions | Which robustness questions are worth space? |
| Notation and examples | What makes the model easier to reconstruct? |
| Scope | How are limitations stated without repeated disclaimers? |

Transfer ordering and explanatory technique, not sentences, mannerisms, or arbitrary length. A pattern in one paper is an option. A repeated pattern is useful only if it solves the present reader's problem.

## Design the title, abstract, and introduction

Use a title that identifies the economic object, question, institution, or mechanism. Include a technical term only when it helps the intended reader distinguish the contribution. Avoid promotional adjectives and internal proof terminology.

Make the abstract answer, in a natural order appropriate to the paper:

- What economic question is studied?
- What does the model add or alter?
- What is the principal result?
- Why does it arise?
- What is its essential scope or implication?

Do not reduce the contribution to "we develop a model" or "we characterize equilibrium." Avoid a fixed word count unless the target venue imposes one.

Open the introduction with the economic problem, institution, fact, or theoretical tension rather than a generic claim of importance or a literature gap. Give enough of the environment to make the result intelligible, state the result and mechanism in economic language, and then position it against the nearest work. Use a customized roadmap only when it helps the reader anticipate a non-obvious architecture.

Organize related literature around the decisions needed to locate the contribution. Compare papers by question, mechanism, environment, solution concept, and result. Avoid one-sentence inventories and claims of novelty based only on absence of an identical model.

## Report the positioning evidence

Match the depth of the report to the user's request. For a substantive literature or venue task, provide:

- the closest sources, their versions, and stable links;
- the comparison dimensions that actually bear on the claim;
- the narrow contribution difference the evidence supports;
- sources or claims that remain inaccessible or unverified;
- the search scope and the point at which additional reading stopped changing the decision.

Label source facts, editorial inferences, and author judgments separately. Do not let a comparison matrix replace the explanatory prose the user requested.

## Use craft sources as starting points, not rules

Useful general guides include William Thomson's [*The Young Person's Guide to Writing Economic Theory*](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.37.1.157) and Simon Board and Moritz Meyer-ter-Vehn's [*Writing Economic Theory Papers*](https://www.econ.ucla.edu/sboard/teaching/writingeconomictheory.pdf). Use them to generate questions about contribution, structure, notation, examples, and proofs. Let the present model, evidence from close papers, and current venue instructions determine the editorial decision.

For each proposed change, retain a short private note:

```text
published practice or precise comparison -> problem in this manuscript -> proposed change
```

Do not cite a craft guide in the manuscript merely because it informed the editing process.

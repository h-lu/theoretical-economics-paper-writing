# Subfield-Specific Checks

Read this reference only when a field-specific model audit or exposition decision is needed. Apply the relevant sections, not every item mechanically. The model itself remains the source of truth.

## Games and strategic interaction

- Specify players, actions or strategies, histories, information sets, payoffs, and the order of moves.
- Distinguish pure, mixed, and behavioral strategies; strategy or realization equivalence from equality of induced outcomes; and mutual best responses from sequential rationality.
- State admissible deviations and whether beliefs and refinements restrict off-path behavior.
- For repeated or dynamic games, state monitoring, public or private histories, commitment, discounting, and continuation values.
- Do not use equilibrium refinement as a neutral tie-breaker; explain which outcomes it removes and why that matters economically.

## Mechanism design, market design, and matching

- State the designer's information, objective, commitment power, feasible mechanism class, and participation assumptions.
- Name the exact incentive concept: dominant-strategy, ex post, Bayesian, interim, or another notion. Keep truth-telling conditions aligned with transfers and timing.
- Use a revelation principle only after verifying its environment, message space, commitment, and equilibrium assumptions.
- Separate allocation feasibility, incentive compatibility, individual rationality, budget balance, stability, and implementability.
- For matching, define preferences, priorities, capacities, contracts, blocking objects, stability notion, and whether the result is mechanism-specific or property-specific.
- Distinguish an impossibility theorem from failure of one mechanism or one restricted class.

## Information economics and contract theory

- Identify who knows the type, action, signal, or state; when it becomes known; and what can be contracted on or verified.
- Separate adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling, persuasion, screening, and learning; do not blend their incentive constraints.
- State participation timing, outside options, limited liability, commitment, and renegotiation assumptions.
- Check whether the distribution satisfies the regularity, monotone-likelihood-ratio, single-crossing, or hazard-rate property actually used.
- Distinguish pooling, separating, semi-separating, bunching, and exclusion, and account for off-path beliefs where relevant.
- Explain which rent, distortion, information design, or commitment force drives the result.

## Decision theory, social choice, and axiomatic work

- State the domain, observables, primitive relation or choice object, and whether the representation is ordinal or cardinal.
- Keep completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence, separability, monotonicity, and richness assumptions distinct.
- Separate necessity, sufficiency, independence, characterization, and representation results.
- For social choice, name the preference domain, aggregation rule, strategic behavior, rights or fairness criterion, and population assumptions.
- Do not interpret a representation's convenient functional form as unique economic structure unless the uniqueness theorem supports it.
- Use counterexamples to explain independence and boundary conditions, not only to decorate the appendix.

## Dynamic, macroeconomic, and general-equilibrium theory

- Define states, controls, shocks, laws of motion, expectations, resource constraints, initial conditions, and horizon.
- Distinguish an individual problem from equilibrium price formation and aggregation; verify that conjectured laws of motion are consistent.
- State whether the object is a competitive, recursive, Markov, rational-expectations, stationary, balanced-growth, or transition equilibrium.
- Check transversality, no-Ponzi, terminal, borrowing, occasionally binding, and market-clearing conditions when they affect the result.
- Separate existence, determinacy, local stability, global dynamics, steady-state comparison, and transition-path welfare.
- For heterogeneous-agent or aggregate models, state the distributional state, aggregation result or approximation, and how it evolves.
- For calibrated or computed models, separate analytical propositions from quantitative findings and report equilibrium branch and numerical diagnostics.

## Industrial organization, political economy, and network models

- State the institution that maps individual actions to prices, policies, votes, links, or allocations.
- Distinguish strategic substitution or complementarity from the equilibrium comparative static it helps produce.
- For entry, pricing, auctions, platforms, or networks, state participation, tie-breaking, market boundaries, and equilibrium selection.
- For political models, separate voter, candidate, legislator, lobby, and policymaker objectives; state commitment and information assumptions.
- Do not infer empirical market power, causal effects, or institutional realism solely from a theoretical equilibrium result.

## Mixed theory-empirical or quantitative papers

- Identify whether theory explains a fact, disciplines an estimand, supplies identifying variation, maps parameters to data, or supports a counterfactual.
- Map each empirical object to a model object and each tested prediction to the proposition that generates it.
- Distinguish a calibrated parameter, an estimated parameter, a normalized object, and an externally assigned value.
- Keep empirical evidence for a channel separate from proof that the model's mechanism is uniquely responsible.
- State which theory claims survive without the empirical exercise and which empirical conclusions require the model.
- Hand the identification, estimation, and data-validity audit to an empirical-economics workflow; keep this skill responsible for the theory component and its interface with evidence.
